* Power supply

C99 and C100 are each 3-section cans (schematic shows them as discrete
20µF/350V sections cascaded down the B+ filter chain — raw 340V rail,
through R116 1K/4W to one node, through R117 2K/8W to the next —
rather than three sections all tapping the same node). Physically
each can has 4 terminals: one common/negative lug plus one lug per
section. Confirmed 2026-07-28 with a meter, not just wire color: the
black-wire lug on both C99 and C100 reads continuity to chassis
ground, matching a nearby tie-point strip's grounded center lug. The
other three lugs on each can (orange wires) are the individual section
taps and should NOT be assumed grounded — see [[file:../log/2026-07-28-first-teardown.org][today's log]] for the
discharge procedure this informed.

S9 (line voltage selector) has a plate screwed to the outside of the
chassis that physically prevents it from sliding, printed "115V" on
the visible face. Confirmed original, not a field addition: the other
side of the plate reads "230V," and the chassis shows uniform patina
all around it with a shiny, protected patch underneath — consistent
with the plate having been mounted since near-manufacture, flipped to
the correct face for the export market rather than added later.

D3/D4 (SD-1A ×2) physically identified 2026-07-28: the two green
diodes in the bottom-close-up-06 cluster. Per the schematic, D4 goes
to ground and D3 goes to C101's hot ("+") side. DMM-confirmed: the
lower diode in the photo is tied to the same grounded point as C102's
negative terminal (C102 being the rear-panel-closest can) — so that
diode is D4, not D3 as first guessed. This matches the manual's
physical layout diagram exactly (C102 nearest the rear panel); there
is no manual erratum here, unlike what was logged earlier. The other
diode, tied directly to D4, is D3, and should land on C101's "+" —
not yet independently confirmed which physical terminal that is.

Wire colors at T9: the red wire from the top of T9 is the 130V AC
winding's hot lead (feeds D3/D4) — a different wire than the red that
goes to S9 (a primary lead). The black wire from the top of T9 ties
to C102's *positive* terminal — a different terminal than D4's ground
connection to C102's negative side. This completes a standard
series-doubler picture: D3 → C101(+); C101(−)/C102(+) → the series
junction, where the black wire from T9 lands; C102(−) → ground, where
D4 lands. Confirms wire colors repeat across different windings on
this transformer, not unique per lead.

T9 lead tracing, 2026-07-28: primary is 4 wires — white, orange,
yellow, red — confirmed by all four landing directly on S9 (which
only switches the primary). White+orange pair opposite yellow+red at
the switch, consistent with two 115V half-windings wired parallel for
115V or series for 230V. All four primary leads exit the same
physical side of T9, alongside two more wires (blue, green) that are
NOT primary — they go elsewhere, presumably secondary. The other side
of T9 has 4 more wires: 2×blue, black, red — also secondary. So
leads aren't split cleanly primary-side/secondary-side on this
transformer; S9's known function was the reliable way to identify the
primary leads, not physical position.

Secondary winding count doesn't obviously reconcile: the schematic
shows ~5 secondary functions (6.3V/4.8A heater, a separate 6.3V/3.6A
heater, V1's and V2's individual hum-balance heater taps via VR5/VR6,
and the 130V AC winding feeding D3/D4), but only 6 secondary wires
exist total. Likely one wire is a shared common return across
multiple windings rather than each winding having its own independent
pair — not yet confirmed by resistance grouping.

Heater hum-balance components, clarified 2026-07-28: VR5 and VR6
(100Ω, at H11 on the schematic) are adjustable hum-balance pots for
V1's and V2's heater supply specifically — *not* cathode-related
despite the "(K)" symbol next to each. Checked directly: V1/V2's own
cathode circuits (tile-C1-E3) show no matching "(K)" symbol, just a
plain cathode resistor to ground (e.g. R9 3k, 0.6V at V1) — an order
of magnitude different from the "5.5V 0.3A" spec at VR5/VR6, which is
a heater figure (6.3V winding sagging under load), not cathode bias.
So "(K)" isn't used consistently for "cathode" across this sheet — it
does mean cathode where it appears directly at V6-V9's own symbols in
the output stage (next to real cathode resistors/bypass caps at
sensible small voltages like 11V), but at H11 it's something else,
possibly an arbitrary connector label in the original draftsman's
convention.

A third, physically-confirmed but not-yet-located-on-the-schematic
heater-balance component exists at V8: a fixed wire-wound resistor
(ends on V8 pins 4/5 — heater/filament, confirmed via EL84 datasheet)
with an adjustable center tap (sliding clamp) grounded at the same
local point C94/C95 also reference. Wiring is dressed under other
factory wiring, consistent with an original component, not a field
addition. Same function as VR5/VR6 (artificial center-tap for hum
reduction) but fixed/grounded rather than adjustable/cathode-routed,
and serving an output tube rather than a preamp tube. Not found on
the schematic despite checking the output/driver and power supply
tiles — possibly a running production change not reflected in this
printed revision.

V1/V2 aren't a phono-specific stage — see [[*Phono EQ][Phono EQ]] below: they're the
shared first-gain stage for whichever input S1 selects. They warrant
the extra hum-balance care regardless of source because they handle
the lowest signal levels in the receiver (phono mag 2.7mV, tape head
1.8mV, vs. 200mV for aux) — hum picked up here gets amplified by
everything downstream, making this the most hum-sensitive point in
the chain independent of which input is active.

* Phono EQ

Not a distinct physical block on this sheet. The phono jacks (PHONO
MAG, PHONO CER/X-TAL) feed a plain resistor divider — R1/R3/R5 left
channel, R2/R4/R6 right — straight into the shared preamp tubes (V1/
V2). A resistor divider alone is frequency-flat; it can't be doing
RIAA correction by itself. Wherever the actual equalization happens
(feedback around V1/V2, or folded into the tone-control network at
R25/R27/R29/R30) hasn't been traced yet.

** Background: what RIAA equalization is, for when this gets traced

The general term is RIAA equalization (or de-emphasis/correction) —
the inverse of the pre-emphasis applied when the record was cut.
Two physical constraints drive the curve:
- Bass is cut on the way in: full-amplitude bass would need groove
  excursions large enough to swing into neighboring grooves. Cut
  before cutting, boost back on playback.
- Treble is boosted on the way in: surface noise is roughly flat or
  worse at high frequencies. Boosting treble before cutting and
  cutting it back down on playback drags noise in that band down
  with it — same trick as FM broadcast pre-emphasis or Dolby B/C.

Net curve: boost bass, cut treble on playback. Defined by three time
constants — 3180µs (~50Hz), 318µs (~500Hz), 75µs (~2.1kHz).

Standardized by RIAA in 1954; the US industry converged on it through
the mid-to-late 1950s. Before that, Columbia, RCA Victor, Decca (ffrr)
and others each ran incompatible curves, which is why earlier-'50s
preamps often had a multi-position equalization selector switch. By
1966 RIAA was the only standard in use — consistent with this
receiver having no curve-select switch, just a fixed network.

Typical circuit: not a passive filter (lossy, harder to hit the curve
precisely) but frequency-dependent negative feedback around a gain
stage — less feedback at low frequencies (more gain, bass boost),
more feedback at high frequencies (less gain, treble cut). Minimum
2 resistors + 2 capacitors in the feedback path realize the three
time constants; tube designs often add one more capacitor purely to
block DC bias current from the feedback path, unrelated to the curve
itself. Exact topology needs deriving/verifying against the traced
circuit when this comes up — not assumed from a textbook example.

* Output stage

V6-V9 are 6BQ5/EL84 (standard 9-pin) — pin 7 is the plate on this
tube type, fixed by construction, not something that needs measuring.

T7/T8 (output transformers, AT6123U) wiring traced 2026-07-28:
- Primary side, three wires: red, brown, green. Red confirmed to V6
  pin 7, green confirmed to V7 pin 7 (both plates, traced directly to
  the tube socket) — so red/green are the two plate leads and brown
  is the center tap by elimination.
- Secondary side, three wires: blue, white, yellow — taps feeding S7
  (impedance selector). Not individually traced to specific ohm taps
  yet.
- These colors are this transformer's own convention, not assumed
  from a general (e.g. EIA) color code — confirm by tracing/measuring
  on any similar part rather than reusing this key elsewhere.

Schematic confirms T7 and T8's primary center taps are the *same
electrical node* — a single continuous trace with a junction dot
ties them together (tile-C10-E12, dot between the two transformers),
one shared B+ feed for both channels' output stage, matching the
335V figure at V6's plate. Physical access is asymmetric: T8's brown
wire and the tie point were visible enough to trace directly, but
T7's red/green/brown group is buried under other wiring and flat
caps, so the brown-to-brown connection wasn't directly confirmed at
the T7 end — inferred from the schematic and partial tracing at T8,
not fully visually verified at both ends. Continuity check between
T7's and T8's brown wires (expect a dead short) would close this out
definitively.

No-load damage check, done 2026-07-28 (both transformers still wired
in-circuit, DMM resistance):
- T8: brown-red 122Ω, brown-green 122Ω (primary halves matched).
  Primary-to-secondary climbed slowly toward ~21kΩ rather than
  settling — consistent with the in-circuit cathode bypass caps
  (C53/C54) and ground returns charging under the meter's test
  current, not a clean isolation reading, since the transformer isn't
  lifted out of circuit. Not the low/stable reading that would
  indicate arc damage.
- T7: brown-red 127Ω, brown-green 128Ω (matched). Same slow-climb
  primary-to-secondary behavior as T8.
- Both transformers read consistent with each other and show no
  signature of shorted turns or insulation breakdown. A fully clean
  primary/secondary isolation reading would require lifting the leads
  out of circuit — not done, not judged necessary given these
  results. Real confirmation still comes at Phase 6 first power-up.

R77/R78 (100Ω composition) and R79/R80 (wirewound, printed 16Ω —
manual's parts list says 10Ω, physical part disagrees, trusting the
physical marking) identified 2026-07-29. R77/R78 confirmed by color
code (brown-black-brown-silver) and continuity to the headphone jack.
R79/R80 confirmed by position: they tap in at the S7-to-S8 junction,
*before* S8 (speakers on/off), not on the speaker-terminal side.

This answers the standing question about S8 and no-load risk: with
S8 switched off, T7/T8's secondary is not actually open — it still
sees a real path through R79/R80 (16Ω) in series with R77/R78 (100Ω),
roughly 116Ω, via the headphone circuit. Higher than the normal 8-16Ω
speaker load, but a finite load, not an open secondary — the
distinction the "never power up with the output transformer secondary
open" standing rule actually cares about. Holds only if R77-R80 are
themselves intact; an independent failure in any of the four would
reopen the no-load risk.

* Selector switch

S1 (input selector) isn't a single localized block — it's drawn as
four disconnected wafer sections spread from C1 to G7: "4/4F"/"4/4R"
near the input jacks, "2/4" and "3/4" wafers in the tone-control area
(D5-E9), "1/4" wafer out near G5-G7 (tuner-band-related switching).
No single zone describes it.
