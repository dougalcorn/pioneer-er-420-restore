Outside perspective — forum advice, other people's experience, things
worth weighing that aren't a measurement or a part spec. Not a task

* Test oil-paper caps individually rather than blanket-replacing

Source: Dandy, Audiokarma reply, 2026-07-29. "Yes, the PIO caps can
be leaky, but all aren't. And the old electrolytics can also be good
after many years. Try not to change too much till you are confident
you know what really needs repair."

Status: open, unresolved against an existing decision. bom.org
currently has all 14 oil-paper designators (C7, C8, C13, C14, C19,
C20, C25, C26, C47, C48, C56, C60, C70, C96) marked for blanket
replacement, on the theory that oil-impregnated paper is a
known-bad-by-construction failure mode (see
[[file:log/2026-07-25-parts-research.org][parts-research.org]]) — not
a "maybe" the way old electrolytics are. Dandy's challenge is fair:
testing in-circuit first (the way Phase 2 already plans to test the
Mylars) would catch which ones are actually still fine, at the cost
of more bench time and more handling of 60-year-old solder joints per
cap kept in. Worth a deliberate decision either way, not a default.

* Restoration can strip a unit's "magic"

Source: Dandy, Audiokarma reply, 2026-07-29. Restored a Pioneer SX-82
fully and never liked the result; picked up a Pioneer SA-400 amp
needing no work at all and liked its sound as-is.

Status: noted, not actionable. Unmeasurable and subjective, but worth
remembering as a reason not to fix something that isn't actually
broken just because it's original and old — a check against
over-restoring corners of the set that already work fine.

* Long-term component ID tracking isn't standard practice

Source: gadget73, Audiokarma reply, 2026-07-31, asked directly how he
tracks which physical component is which while working. "Personally I
don't usually bother but if i have to order a bunch of parts, I will
use Mousers customer part number field to ID the new parts, then put
the old in the bag with that number. If there are 6 identical caps I
don't label each one though. If I'm working out of my parts stock, bad
parts just go into a bag or other container and once I'm done they go
in the trash."

Status: agree, and already the de facto practice here without anyone
stating it as a rule. He's answering from an experienced-tech,
fast-turnaround context — components are usually interchangeable once
identified, so nothing to track long-term. This project's reason for
[[file:component-notes.org][component-notes.org]] is different (beginner, first-time
verification work, don't want to redo it after walking away for
months) but the scoping instinct matches exactly: every component that
got the deep-dive treatment tonight (R79/R80, C13/C14, C96, T7/T8) had
a real question attached, not just "label it because it exists" — the
four identical C49-C52 Mylars got confirmed-same-spec-and-clean, not
individually distinguished. Worth adopting directly: the Mouser
customer-PN-field + bagged-old-part trick, once Phase 6 pulling parts
actually starts, as a cheap physical complement to the digital
tracking rather than a replacement for it.

* In-circuit ESR meter, not just continuity, for cap testing

Source: Novalux Stereophonic, "What 1000+ Repairs Taught Me About
Recapping Vintage Receivers," 2026-05-19.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7X8sE9LF1MY. Demonstrates testing
electrolytics with a Velleman Atlas ESR70 capacitance/ESR meter,
in-circuit, as part of a break-fix-first methodology: baseline test
everything before deciding what actually needs replacing, rather than
replacing on age alone. Also notes that over 90% of pulled parts on a
typical restoration test technically still good, and that failure
correlates more with construction era/type and heat exposure than
raw age — 1960s multi-section can electrolytics specifically get no
benefit of the doubt ("I don't really trust any of them at this
point"), which is a point in favor of Phase 5's planned discrete
rebuild of C99/C100 rather than a reason to reconsider it.

Status: open, actionable. Every capacitor test logged so far (Mylars,
in-circuit resistor sweep) has been continuity/resistance only via
DMM — real capacitance and ESR readings aren't something the current
toolkit can produce. That's directly relevant to the still-open
[[*Test oil-paper caps individually rather than blanket-replacing][oil-paper testing question]] above: an ESR meter would let that survey
actually measure leakage/degradation in place rather than relying on
continuity alone, which only catches a dead short, not a leaky or
drifted cap. Worth deciding whether to buy one before Phase 5/7
testing starts in earnest, or treat continuity-only as good enough
for this unit's scope.

* Recap paper and electrolytic on sight; leave mica/ceramic alone

Source: antiqueradio.org, "Recapping Old Radios and TVs," retrieved
2026-08-14. https://www.antiqueradio.org/recap.htm. Paper/molded-paper
and electrolytic caps: always replace. Mica and ceramic: "extremely
reliable," rarely worth touching. Failure mode for paper caps is
leakage from water vapor penetrating the wax/coating over decades, not
outright shorting. Also: replace one capacitor at a time and
double-check each against the schematic before moving to the next;
match or exceed original voltage rating (250V→450V called out
explicitly as fine); non-electrolytics should stay within ~20% of
original capacitance, electrolytics can go up to 100% higher.

Status: agree, and mostly already the de facto practice here rather
than new advice. The oil-paper/electrolytic-only split matches
bom.org's own strategy and README.org's stated plan exactly; "one at a
time, check against schematic" matches CLAUDE.md's standing rule
already. Two pieces are more than just confirmation, though: the
capacitance-tolerance numbers (~20% for film/ceramic, up to 100% for
electrolytics) are a concrete rule of thumb this project hasn't
written down anywhere, and they directly bear on the still-open C600
replacement decision (2026-08-12/13 log) — 100µF against a 60µF
original is exactly at this article's stated electrolytic ceiling, so
that choice has outside backing, not just gadget73's rough parallel-cap
math. Second, this is a real counterweight to the still-open
[[*Test oil-paper caps individually rather than blanket-replacing][Dandy entry above]] arguing for individual testing before replacement
— this source leans the opposite way, treating paper-cap leakage as
inevitable-by-construction rather than a "maybe." Doesn't resolve that
open question on its own, but it's a second, independent voice on the
blanket-replace side of it.

* Standard restoration order: inspect, test tubes, order parts, power supply first, dim-bulb power-up

Source: antiqueradio.org, "First Steps in Restoration," retrieved
2026-08-14. https://www.antiqueradio.org/FirstStepsInRestoration.htm.
Never power up an unrestored radio without preliminary checks — "may
destroy expensive parts or start a fire, and it won't tell you
anything very useful." Get the schematic first. Remove chassis, clean
with isopropyl alcohol (not water submersion), inspect for obvious
damage, test every tube (filament continuity by hand if no tester).
Then: order replacement parts (especially caps) while working
cosmetics in parallel, replace power-supply electrolytics first,
power up cautiously on a variac or dim-bulb limiter, address remaining
issues against that baseline. Also: "most of the time, most of your
tubes will still be usable," and extensive testing before recapping is
wasteful — systematic replacement of known failure-prone categories
beats chasing certainty on each individual part.

Status: agree, and this is closer to a structural sanity-check than
new advice — the restoration plan's own phase order (schematic prep,
then access/make-safe, then survey including tube inventory, parts
ordering throughout, Phase 5 power supply first, Phase 6 dim-bulb
power-up) already matches this sequence almost exactly, arrived at
independently rather than copied from this article. Good sign the plan
isn't missing a step. One new, concrete, actionable detail this
project hasn't written down: isopropyl alcohol for chassis cleaning
rather than water — worth folding into Phase 3's still-open "blow out
dust" item. Also a third independent voice (after Dandy and the recap
article above) on the still-open
[[*Test oil-paper caps individually rather than blanket-replacing][oil-paper testing question]], this
time explicit that extensive pre-testing is "wasteful" — the
blanket-replace side of that question now has more outside backing
than the individual-testing side, though it's still not been decided
one way or the other on this project's own terms.

* "How Does a Tube Amp Work?" — AudioKarma community FAQ (PDF)

Source: AudioKarma.org community-authored FAQ, saved locally as
How-a-tube-amp-works.pdf. General glossary/primer covering tube
element function (cathode, grid, anode, heater, getter), tube types
(dual-triode preamp tubes vs. pentode power tubes), power transformer
and rectification basics, and — most relevant here — power supply
filter caps vs. coupling caps (polar/parallel vs. non-polar/series,
with a clean explanation from @GordonW at AudioKarma: caps are a short
for AC and open for DC, so a supply filter cap shunts ripple to ground
while a coupling cap blocks DC and passes signal), plus fixed vs.
self/cathode biasing for power tubes. Directly cites and links the
same antiqueradio.org recap.htm article already logged above.

Status: reference, not advice to weigh — same category as the Uncle
Doug videos above, general theory rather than a position on this
project's decisions. The cathode-biasing section is the most directly
relevant part given this week's C600 investigation (shared cathode-bias
network, corner-frequency reasoning) — worth rereading that section
specifically if the R9/V1 double-cathode-path question from the
2026-08-11 log ever gets picked back up.
